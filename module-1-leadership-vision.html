Part One

Why Leadership & Vision Matter

⏱️ 10 minutes
+

Understanding Leadership vs Management

Before we dive in, let's clarify something important: management and leadership are not the same thing.

📋
Management
Organises resources, executes plans, maintains systems
🌟
Leadership
Inspires people, creates purpose, drives change

As Peter Drucker said: "Management is doing things right; leadership is doing the right things." You need both, but in this module we're focusing on the leadership side—the vision that gives your management work meaning.

At Indigo Luna right now: We're in a period of significant change—warehouse consolidation, design team transitions, fulfilment migration, new store openings. Your team doesn't just need task management. They need to understand WHY these changes matter and WHERE we're heading. That's leadership.

A Scenario to Consider

You're leading the Bali warehouse team during the upcoming consolidation to Buduk. The team is anxious about the move—new location, new workflows, uncertainty about their roles.

You have a choice: Focus on managing the logistics (task lists, timelines, processes), OR lead with vision (why this move matters, what it unlocks for the team and the business, where you're all heading together).

Pause and think: If you were a warehouse team member, which approach would help you feel more confident and motivated about this major change?

Core Concept: Vision Provides Direction

Think of vision as your team's North Star. When sailors navigated the ocean centuries ago, they didn't have GPS—they had the North Star. It didn't tell them exactly where to go, but it gave them orientation. It helped them know if they were moving in the right direction.

That's what vision does for your team. It doesn't micromanage every decision, but it gives everyone a shared sense of direction.

What Happens Without Vision?

When teams lack a clear vision, three things happen:

  • Disorganisation — Without a North Star, everyone has different ideas of what's important. Priorities clash. Energy gets scattered.
  • Disconnection — People show up, do their tasks, go home. But they don't feel part of something bigger. Work becomes transactional, not meaningful.
  • Reactivity — The team responds to whatever's loudest or most urgent, rather than proactively moving toward a goal. You're always putting out fires instead of building something.
At Indigo Luna, our vision isn't just to sell yoga wear—it's to build a sustainable brand that respects people and planet while creating beautiful, intentional products. Every department, every role, every task connects to that vision.

Pause and reflect: Think about the last time you felt truly motivated at work. What was happening? Chances are, you weren't just completing tasks—you felt connected to a bigger purpose.

5 Reasons Vision Matters for Your Team

1. Vision Provides Direction

When your team faces a decision—big or small—vision acts as a filter. "Does this move us closer to where we're going, or further away?" Without vision, decisions are made based on ease, urgency, or personal preference. With vision, decisions align.

2. Vision Brings Ideas to Life

Anyone can have a vision. But a leader translates that vision into action. You're the bridge between "where we want to go" and "what we do today." You communicate it, model it, and help your team see how their daily work connects to the bigger picture.

3. Vision Builds Purpose

Research consistently shows that people don't leave jobs just for more money—they leave because they don't feel their work matters. A compelling vision gives people a reason to care. It transforms "I'm just packing boxes" into "I'm part of delivering sustainable fashion to customers who share our values."

4. Vision Navigates Change

When challenges arise—and they always do—vision keeps teams grounded. Instead of seeing a setback as a failure, you see it as a detour on the journey. At Indigo Luna, we're navigating major changes this year. The teams that thrive won't be the ones that avoid challenges, but the ones who understand WHY we're pushing through them.

5. Vision Creates Movements, Not Just Tasks

The difference between a job and a movement is belief. When people believe in where they're going, they don't just show up—they contribute, innovate, advocate. Vision turns employees into advocates and teams into communities.

Reflect for a moment: Which of these five reasons resonates most with your department right now? Where do you see the biggest opportunity?
Example from Indigo Luna:
When we opened our first Bali store, the vision wasn't "sell more product." It was "create a space where customers feel the soul of the brand—warmth, sustainability, connection." That vision shaped the stores: the natural materials we chose for fixtures, the way staff were trained to greet customers, even the music playing in the background. Vision isn't abstract—it becomes concrete through a thousand small decisions.
Part Two

What Is a Vision?

⏱️ 10 minutes
+

Definition

A vision is a clear, aspirational statement that defines the long-term purpose and desired impact of your team or department.

Key Characteristics:

Simon Sinek's "Start with Why"

Simon Sinek discovered something powerful when studying the world's most inspiring leaders and organisations: they all communicate in the same pattern. But it's the opposite of how most businesses communicate.

The Golden Circle

Most organisations communicate from the outside in:

  1. WHAT we do (we sell yoga wear)
  2. HOW we do it (sustainable materials, Bali-made)
  3. WHY we do it (our purpose and belief)

This is logical and clear, but it doesn't inspire action. It doesn't make people care.

But great leaders start with WHY. They communicate from the inside out: Why we exist → How we do it → What we make.

Why Starting with WHY Works

When you start with WHAT, you're speaking to the rational brain—the part that processes facts and features. When you start with WHY, you're speaking to the emotional brain—the part that drives decision-making, loyalty, and behaviour.

Example:

  • Starting with WHAT: "We make sustainable yoga wear from organic materials. We have stores in Bali and ship worldwide. You should buy from us because we're eco-friendly."
  • Starting with WHY: "We believe that business should be a force for good—honouring people, planet, and craft. That's why we source sustainable materials and work with Bali artisans. We make yoga wear that reflects those values."

Which one makes you feel something? Which one would you want to be part of?

Indigo Luna's Golden Circle:

WHY: We believe that business can be a force for good—honouring people, planet, and craft while creating products that make people feel intentional and connected.

HOW: We design timeless, sustainable pieces with Bali artisans, using ethical production and eco-conscious materials.

WHAT: We create yoga wear and lifestyle products for conscious consumers.
Think about it: When you talk about your department's work, do you usually start with WHAT you do, or WHY it matters? Most of us default to WHAT because it feels more concrete. But WHY is what creates connection.

Part Three

SMART Goal Setting

⏱️ 10 minutes
+

The Problem: Tasks vs Results

Here's a common trap: a manager sets a goal like "Hold weekly team meetings" or "Send monthly reports." These feel like goals, but they're actually just tasks—activities you'll do. They don't tell you what success looks like.

TASK = Action you perform
Example: "Check inventory levels every morning"

RESULT = Outcome you achieve
Example: "Maintain 98% inventory accuracy to reduce order delays"

The task tells you what to DO. The result tells you what CHANGES because you did it. Tasks are important—they're how you get to results. But if you only focus on tasks, you miss the point.

Why This Matters

If your goal is "hold weekly team meetings," you could have terrible, unproductive meetings every week and technically hit your goal. But if your goal is "improve team alignment and reduce miscommunication," then the weekly meeting becomes a tool to achieve that result—and you'll naturally make those meetings better because you're focused on the outcome.

Great leaders focus on results, not just tasks. When you review your team's work, don't just ask "Did we do the thing?" Ask "Did the thing work? What changed?"
Indigo Luna Example:
Task-focused goal: "Post to Instagram 5 times per week."
Result-focused goal: "Increase Instagram engagement rate from 2.5% to 4% by end of Q2 through consistent, high-quality content that resonates with our audience."

See the difference? The first one is a checklist item. The second one connects activity to impact—and gives you permission to experiment with WHAT you post, as long as it drives engagement.

SMART Framework: Turning Vague Ideas into Clear Goals

SMART is one of the most widely-used goal-setting frameworks because it works. It forces you to think through the details that make a goal actually achievable.

M
Measurable

A

S — Specific

Vague goals don't get done. "Improve team communication" could mean a hundred different things. But "Hold weekly 30-minute team meetings to share updates and surface blockers" is something you can actually do.

Ask yourself: Could someone else read this goal and know exactly what success looks like?

❌ "Improve team communication"
✅ "Hold weekly 30-minute team meetings every Monday at 9am to share updates, surface blockers, and align on priorities"

M — Measurable

If you can't measure it, you can't manage it. "Increase engagement" is hopeful. "Increase Instagram engagement rate from 2.5% to 4%" is a target you can track.

Ask yourself: How will I know if I've succeeded? What metric will change?

❌ "Increase social media engagement"
✅ "Increase Instagram engagement rate from 2.5% to 4% by end of Q2"

A — Achievable

Goals should stretch you, not break you. If your goal requires resources you don't have or skills you can't develop in time, it's not achievable—it's a wish. Be ambitious but realistic.

Ask yourself: Do I have (or can I get) the resources, skills, and time needed to do this?

❌ "Redesign entire website in 2 weeks with no developer"
✅ "Improve product page UX by implementing 3 research-backed changes this quarter"

R — Relevant

This is where vision comes in. A goal might be achievable and measurable, but if it doesn't ladder up to what actually matters for Indigo Luna, it's a distraction. Every goal should connect to a bigger objective.

Ask yourself: Does this goal support Indigo Luna's priorities? If we achieve this, will it matter?

❌ "Redesign the office kitchen layout" (nice to have, but not strategic)
✅ "Launch new sustainable product line to support our 35% revenue growth target" (directly supports business goals)

T — Time-bound

A goal without a deadline is a dream. Deadlines create urgency and focus. "Someday" never comes. "By March 31" does.

Ask yourself: When will this be done? What's the deadline?

❌ "Complete customer satisfaction survey eventually"
✅ "Launch customer satisfaction survey by March 15 and analyse results by March 31"

Common pitfall: People sometimes make goals SO specific and rigid that there's no room to adapt. SMART goals should guide you, not trap you. If circumstances change, revise the goal—but do it intentionally, not as an excuse.

Part Four

Communicating Vision Through Storytelling

⏱️ 10 minutes
+

Why Storytelling Works

You've probably been in meetings where someone presents data: slides full of charts, metrics, bullet points. You nod along. Then someone tells a story about a customer, a challenge the team faced, a moment that mattered—and suddenly everyone leans in.

Why? Because our brains are wired for stories.

Neuroscience research shows that when we hear facts, only the language-processing parts of our brain activate. But when we hear a story, our entire brain lights up—the same parts that would activate if we were experiencing the story ourselves. Stories create neural coupling—your brain syncs with the storyteller's brain.

💭
Stories are memorable
People remember stories 22x more than facts alone
❤️
Create emotional connection
Stories trigger empathy and trust
✨
Make visions tangible
Turn abstract ideas into concrete images

As a leader, storytelling is one of your most powerful tools. When you tell a story about why a change matters, or how a customer was impacted, or what success could look like, you're not just informing your team—you're inviting them into a shared experience.

Important: Storytelling isn't manipulation. It's connection. You're not making things up—you're highlighting the human truth behind the work. You're making the invisible visible.

The 5-Step Storytelling Framework for Leaders

Here's a simple structure you can use anytime you need to communicate vision, inspire action, or help your team see the bigger picture.

1. Start with Why (Connect to Purpose First)

Before you tell people WHAT to do or HOW to do it, ground them in WHY it matters. Connect the work to something bigger than the immediate task.

The shift: Instead of leading with the problem or the ask, lead with the purpose.

❌ "We need to be more sustainable." (task-focused, vague)
✅ "Every year, the fashion industry creates millions of tons of waste. We have the power to change that by designing timeless pieces that last. That's why sustainability isn't a nice-to-have for us—it's core to who we are." (purpose-focused, compelling)

2. Use Personal or Relatable Stories

Abstract concepts don't move people. Specific moments do. Share a real story—something you witnessed, a customer you met, a challenge the team faced. Make it concrete.

The shift: Instead of explaining a concept, show it through a moment.

Example: "When I first visited our Bali production site and saw artisans hand-stitching each piece with care, I realised—this isn't just fashion. This is culture. This is tradition. This is someone's livelihood and craft. That moment changed how I talk about what we do."

3. Make the TEAM the Hero (Not You)

The best stories position the team as the protagonist. You're the guide, but they're the ones on the journey. Use "we" language, not "I" language. Help them see their role in the bigger story.

The shift: Instead of "I need you to…" use "We have an opportunity to…"

❌ "I want us to grow revenue this year." (leader-centred)
✅ "Right now, we're a small brand making a big difference. Imagine a future where every sustainable piece we create helps shift the industry towards ethics and intention. That's the journey WE'RE on together. And what we do this quarter shapes where we go next." (team-centred)

4. Make it Visual & Emotional

Use metaphors, sensory language, and vivid descriptions. Help people see the vision, not just understand it intellectually.

The shift: Instead of abstract language, use images and feelings.

Example: "Think of Indigo Luna like a tree. We're rooted in tradition—honouring craft, sustainability, the land we work on. We're growing towards innovation—new designs, new markets, new possibilities. And like a tree, we give back to the world—through ethical practices, community support, environmental care."

5. End with a Clear Call to Action

Don't leave people inspired but directionless. Connect the story to what happens next. What's the one thing they should do, think, or feel as a result?

The shift: Instead of ending with inspiration alone, end with invitation.

Example: "Each of us plays a role in this story. Whether you're packing orders, designing the next collection, or supporting a customer through a question—YOU are shaping the future of conscious fashion. So this week, I want you to think: How does my work connect to that bigger vision? And where can I make an even greater impact?"
Practice tip: You don't need to be a natural storyteller to use this framework. Start small. Next time you're in a team meeting, instead of jumping straight to the agenda, spend 60 seconds telling a story about WHY the work matters. Notice what shifts.

Part Five

Vision Statement Exercise

⏱️ 15 minutes
+

The Framework: Draft → Actionable → Inspiring

You're going to create a vision statement for YOUR department at Indigo Luna. Not a generic statement you copy from somewhere else, but one that genuinely reflects where your team is going and why it matters.

We'll build it in three passes, refining each time. Don't try to make it perfect on the first try—that's not how good writing works.

Step 1: Draft an Initial Vision Statement

Start broad. What is the ultimate impact of your department's work? Not the tasks you do, but the change you create. If your department disappeared tomorrow, what would Indigo Luna lose?

Tips for this step:

  • Don't overthink it—just get something down
  • Focus on impact, not activity
  • It's okay if it feels generic at this stage
Example (Warehouse):
"Our department will make warehouse operations more efficient and sustainable."

This is a decent start—it identifies the outcome (efficiency + sustainability) but it's not specific yet. That's fine for Step 1.

Step 2: Make It Actionable

Now add the HOW. What's your approach? What makes your department's vision unique? Generic visions like "we will be the best" don't help anyone. Specific approaches like "by implementing smart automation and sustainable packaging" give direction.

Tips for this step:

  • Think about your department's unique strengths or methods
  • Be specific enough that someone could understand your strategy
  • Connect to Indigo Luna's values (sustainability, people, quality)
Example (Warehouse - Refined):
"By implementing smart automation, optimising workflows, and reducing packaging waste, we will create warehouse operations that maximise efficiency while minimising environmental impact."

Now it's actionable—you can see the strategy (automation, workflow optimisation, sustainable packaging). This gives the team direction.

Step 3: Make It Inspiring

This is where you connect your department's work to something bigger than itself. Why does this matter beyond your team? How does it serve customers, the business, the planet, the industry?

The best vision statements don't just describe what you'll do—they paint a picture of what becomes possible if you succeed. They make people want to be part of the journey.

Tips for this step:

  • Connect to Indigo Luna's larger mission (sustainability, conscious fashion, people-first business)
  • Think about who benefits (customers, planet, team, industry)
  • Use language that creates a sense of possibility and ambition
  • Ask: If we achieve this vision, what changes in the world?
Example (Warehouse - Final):
"Through smart automation, optimised workflows, and sustainable packaging, we will set new standards in eco-conscious logistics—proving that warehouse operations can be both high-performing and planet-friendly, while delivering seamless experiences for our customers."

Now it's inspiring—it positions the work as pioneering ("set new standards"), connects to values ("eco-conscious"), and shows the dual benefit (efficiency + sustainability). Someone reading this can feel proud to be part of it.
Common mistake: Making the vision so lofty it feels disconnected from reality. Balance aspiration with believability. "We will revolutionise global logistics" feels hollow. "We will set new standards in eco-conscious logistics" feels achievable and inspiring.
