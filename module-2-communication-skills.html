Part One

Why Communication Matters for Managers

10 minutes
+

If Leadership Is Direction, Communication Is the Journey

In Module 1, we explored how vision gives your team direction. But vision alone isn't enough — you need to bring people along on the journey. Communication is how that happens.

As a manager at Indigo Luna, you're navigating genuine complexity: coordinating across Indonesia and Australia, managing teams with diverse cultural backgrounds, and ensuring clarity when you can't always be face-to-face. The quality of your communication directly impacts team performance, morale, and your ability to scale.

Research shows managers spend 70–90% of their time communicating. Yet most receive little formal training in this skill. Communication is your highest-leverage tool — and it's one you can actively improve.

The Cost of Poor Communication

In a fast-growing business like Indigo Luna, poor communication creates compounding problems that are much harder to fix the longer they go unaddressed.

  • Rework and delays: Unclear instructions lead to misaligned work. Teams waste time building the wrong thing, then rebuilding it.
  • Disengagement: When people don't feel heard, they stop contributing ideas. You lose the collective intelligence of your team.
  • Hidden problems: If your team doesn't trust you'll listen, they stop raising concerns. Small issues become crises.
  • Unresolved conflict: Avoided conflict festers. Productivity drops as people spend energy on interpersonal tension instead of their work.

The good news? Communication is a skill you can learn and practise. This module gives you frameworks and techniques you can start using immediately.

Part Two

The 3 Pillars of Effective Communication

10 minutes
+

Great managerial communication rests on three foundational pillars. Master these, and you will handle 90% of situations effectively.

1. Transparency

Sharing context, reasoning, and truth — even when it's uncomfortable. It's the foundation of trust.

2. Active Listening

Fully receiving what someone is saying — not just waiting for your turn to talk. Rare, difficult, and transformative.

3. Conflict Resolution

Resolving disagreements productively so the team can move forward. Conflict is data — it tells you where things are misaligned.

Pillar 1: Transparency

When you explain your reasoning, your team can fill in gaps, catch blind spots, and make better decisions when you're not in the room. When you hide information, people fill the void with anxiety and speculation.

What Transparency Looks Like at Indigo Luna

Instead of: "We need to cut warehouse hours next month."

Try: "Let me share our situation. Revenue dipped 8% last month due to seasonal patterns we are still learning. We need to reduce warehouse hours by 15% in March to stay healthy. Here is the data I am looking at... What questions do you have?"

When to limit transparency: Personal employee issues, strategic decisions still in flux, or sensitive commercial information. The rule: default to transparency unless there's a clear reason not to share.

Your goal: spend more time at Levels 2 and 3. In important moments — one-on-ones, conflict, strategic conversations — aim for Level 2 at minimum. Notice when you drift to Level 1 (listening to respond), take a breath, and return to Level 2 (listening to understand).

Techniques for Active Listening

1. Paraphrasing

Restate what you heard in your own words. This confirms understanding and shows you are truly listening.

Example: "So what I'm hearing is that you feel the new process is adding extra steps without clear benefit to the customer. Is that right?"

2. Clarifying Questions

Ask to dig deeper, not to challenge. Your goal is to understand their thinking, not prove them wrong.

Good: "Can you help me understand what happened after that?"
Bad: "But didn't you already know the deadline?"

3. The Pause

After someone finishes speaking, wait 2–3 seconds before responding. This signals you're processing rather than reacting, gives them space to add more (often the most important things come after the pause), and slows the conversation so you both think more clearly.

Active Listening at Indigo Luna

A warehouse team member: "The new inventory system is causing problems."

Level 1: "We just spent three months on that system. Let me explain how it works..." — You're defending, not listening.

Level 2: "Tell me more about what problems you're seeing." Pause. Listen fully. "So the scanner times out during busy periods, creating a backlog. Is that the core issue?"

Level 3: Same as Level 2, but you also notice: this is the third person this week raising the system. "Have others on your team noticed similar issues? I want to make sure we're hearing from everyone."
Part Four

Asking Powerful Questions

10 minutes
+

Open-Ended vs Closed Questions

The quality of your questions determines the quality of information you receive. As a manager, you need questions that open up thinking rather than shut it down.

Closed questions — answered with "yes," "no," or a single word. Useful for confirming facts; poor for understanding context.

"Did you finish the report?" / "Is the team happy?" / "Who made that decision?"

Problem: You get minimal information and signal you just want a quick answer, not a real conversation.
Open-ended questions — require thought and explanation. They invite context, reasoning, and concerns.

"What is your progress on the report, and what is blocking you?" / "How is the team feeling about the new process?" / "Walk me through how that decision got made."

Benefit: You learn what is really happening, uncover hidden problems, and show you genuinely want to understand.

The Power of "What" and "How"

Start questions with "What" or "How" to naturally create open-ended questions. Avoid "Why" in tense situations — it can feel accusatory.

Why → What: A simple reframe

"Why did you miss the deadline?" — Feels like blame. Puts people on the defensive.

"What got in the way of hitting the deadline?" — Feels like genuine curiosity. Invites honest explanation.

Questions for Different Situations

To understand context: "What is the situation from your perspective?" / "How did this develop?" / "What have you tried so far?"

To uncover root causes: "What do you think is really driving this issue?" / "If we could fix one thing, what would have the biggest impact?"

To encourage ownership: "What do you think we should do?" / "How would you approach this if you were me?"

To test understanding: "Can you tell me what you heard me say?" / "What is still unclear?"

Indigo Luna scenario: Your Indonesia team seems disengaged in video meetings — cameras off, minimal participation.

Weak: "Can everyone please turn cameras on?" — Closed. Doesn't address the root cause.

Powerful: In your next one-on-one: "I've noticed participation in team meetings has been quieter lately. What's your experience of those meetings? What would make them more valuable for you?"
Part Five

Giving Effective Feedback

10 minutes
+

The SBI Method: Situation — Behaviour — Impact

Feedback is how people grow. Yet most managers avoid giving it because it feels awkward, or they give it poorly — vague, emotional, or too late. The SBI Method gives you a simple three-part structure for feedback that is clear, specific, and actionable.

S — Situation

When and where did this happen? Be specific. This grounds the feedback in a real moment, not a vague generalisation.
"In yesterday's team meeting..."

B — Behaviour

What exactly did they do or say? Describe observable actions, not your interpretation. Facts, not judgements.
"...you interrupted Sarah and said 'let's move on.'"

I — Impact

What was the result? Explain the consequence — on you, the team, the work, or the customer.
"Sarah looked discouraged and didn't speak up again."

Full SBI example: "In yesterday's team meeting, when Sarah raised a concern about the new system, you interrupted and said 'let's move on.' Sarah looked discouraged and didn't speak up for the rest of the meeting. I'm concerned her perspective isn't being heard, and we might miss important issues."

Then pause. Listen to their response. This is a conversation, not a lecture.

Positive feedback also uses SBI

Don't just use SBI for problems. Specific praise is far more powerful than a generic "good job."

Positive SBI at Indigo Luna

"In last week's customer complaint [Situation], you stayed calm, listened to their full story without interrupting, and proposed two solutions [Behaviour]. The customer left feeling heard and chose to keep their order — that's exactly the kind of service recovery we need [Impact]. Really well done."

Cultural Sensitivity in Feedback

At Indigo Luna, you're managing across cultures. What feels direct and helpful in Australia may feel harsh or embarrassing in Indonesia, and vice versa.

  • Private, not public: In many cultures (including Indonesia), public criticism causes loss of face. Always give critical feedback one-on-one.
  • Relationship first: Build trust before delivering tough feedback. If someone doesn't believe you care about them, feedback lands as criticism.
  • Invitation, not demand: "I have some thoughts on how that meeting went. Would it be helpful if I shared them?" This gives the person agency.
  • Balance: Pair developmental feedback with recognition of strengths. Don't make the whole conversation about what's wrong.
Timing matters. Give feedback soon after the behaviour (ideally within 24–48 hours) while it's fresh, but not in the heat of the moment. If you're frustrated, wait until you can deliver it calmly.

Pillar 3: Conflict Resolution

The 5 Conflict Management Styles

Conflict is inevitable in any team. The question isn't whether conflict will arise, but how you'll respond. Avoiding conflict doesn't preserve harmony — it allows resentment to build.

The Thomas-Kilmann model identifies five approaches, each suited to different situations. Understanding all five — and knowing your own default — is the foundation of navigating conflict well.

Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Model — 5 Styles mapped by Assertiveness & Cooperation
High assertiveness ↑
🥊 Competing
High assertiveness · Low cooperation
You push your position firmly. Necessary for emergencies — but damages relationships if overused.
Best for: safety issues, ethical violations, urgent decisions.
🌟 Collaborating
High assertiveness · High cooperation
You work together until both parties are fully satisfied. Takes time but builds trust and finds the best solutions.
Best for: important decisions, ongoing relationships, creative solutions.
🤷 Compromising — Middle ground
Medium assertiveness · Medium cooperation. Both parties give up something. Faster than collaborating but less satisfying for both sides. Best when time is short and both sides need to feel heard.
🏃 Avoiding
Low assertiveness · Low cooperation
You withdraw, hoping the conflict resolves itself. Usually delays rather than solves the problem.
Best for: trivial issues, or when you need to cool down first.
🤝 Accommodating
Low assertiveness · High cooperation
You yield to the other person's preferences. Preserves relationships when it matters — but risks being taken advantage of.
Best for: when the issue matters more to them, or you were wrong.
← Low cooperation High cooperation →

Most people have one style they overuse. Some managers avoid conflict until it explodes. Others compete on everything and burn out their teams. The skill is recognising your default and consciously choosing the right style for the situation.

De-escalation Techniques

When conflict is heated, your first job is to lower the temperature so rational conversation is possible.

1. Acknowledge Emotion

Don't ignore or minimise feelings. Name what you're seeing: "I can see this is really frustrating for you." This validation often reduces intensity significantly.

2. Slow Down

Speak more slowly and calmly than the other person. Your tone sets the tone. If you match their intensity, the conflict escalates.

3. Separate People from the Problem

Frame the conflict as "us vs the problem," not "you vs me." Say: "Let's figure this out together" instead of "You need to understand that..."

4. Take a Break if Needed

"This is important and I want to give it the attention it deserves. Can we take 10 minutes and come back to this?" This is not avoiding — it is being strategic.

De-escalation at Indigo Luna

Two team members arguing in the warehouse about a mispacked order. Voices are raised.

Poor response: "Both of you, stop. I don't care whose fault it was, just fix it." — Shuts down without resolving the issue. Problem will repeat.

De-escalation: "Hey — I can see you're both frustrated. Let's pause. I want to understand what happened. Walk me through the process step by step, and let's figure out together how this happened so we can prevent it next time." — You are investigating the process, not assigning blame.
